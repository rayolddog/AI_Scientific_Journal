## Level 1 — For a Non-Specialist

Standard cosmology often describes the Big Bang as a moment ($t = 0$) when all of space, time, and matter sprang out of "nothing" at an infinitely dense point where the laws of physics break down.

This paper explores a physical alternative modeled on a **phase transition**—like a droplet of liquid water condensing inside an infinite cloud of water vapor, or a crystal nucleating inside a supercooled liquid:
1. **Before the Big Bang (The Uncondensed Quantum Vacuum):** Instead of "nothing," there is an infinite, timeless quantum vacuum made of fundamental spinning wave-fields (**Weyl spinors**). Because left-handed and right-handed spinors have not yet locked together, there are no massive particles, no ticking clocks, and no $3+1\text{D}$ ruler or gravity—just an timeless sea of pure quantum waves.
2. **The Big Condensation:** When a random wave fluctuation in this infinite sea concentrates enough energy in one region, it triggers a phase transition: left- and right-handed spinors lock together, creating mass, ticking clocks (proper time), and an expanding bubble of $3+1\text{D}$ spacetime. Because the transition happens across a thin but finite boundary rather than a point of zero size, there is no infinite mathematical singularity at the beginning of time.
3. **Coexistence with the Quantum Vacuum Today & Cosmic Expansion:** Even after our $3+1\text{D}$ bubble formed, the underlying quantum vacuum didn't disappear—our classical world floats inside it and coexists with it. At microscopic scales, we see the quantum vacuum in phenomena like the Casimir force and quantum entanglement. At the largest cosmic scale, the **surface area** of our expanding condensate bubble interacts with the **zero-point energy** of the quantum vacuum ($\rho_\Lambda \sim (\hbar H M_{\text{Pl}})^2$). Because the ratio of a sphere's surface area ($4\pi R_H^2$) to its volume ($\frac{4}{3}\pi R_H^3$) shrinks as $3/R_H$, this surface-area $\times$ zero-point energy mechanism naturally explains why dark energy is tiny rather than $10^{120}$ times too large, and predicts that dark energy slowly evolves over cosmic time—matching recent 2024–2025 DESI galaxy survey measurements.
